## Abstract/text-informed connections for the atlas

These are editorial connections, not proof of collaboration or influence.

- Hewitt's [2024 dissertation summary](https://doi.org/10.1017/eso.2024.38), abstract and introduction read, studies the political reversal by which cartels became prohibited after having served as accepted instruments of economic governance. It connects to questions about public/private power and the state's economic authority; its abstract explicitly treats Western Europe, 1918–1957. The similarly named thesis and developing book must not create three published-book counts.
- Hewitt's [World in Blocs](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1740022822000262), abstract read, examines imperial regionalism through Leo Amery rather than assuming a uniform liberal international order. It can be paired analytically with featured work on informal empire and transimperial histories.
- Balguy's [BnF essay](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/labolition-lesclavage-1848), text read, connects abolition to contested compensation, land, labour and citizenship. The [RetroNews interview](https://www.retronews.fr/colonies/interview/2025/03/24/indemnisation-proprietaires-d-esclaves) explains the heterogeneity of compensated owners. This supports a property/slavery/empire cluster without describing a later CHEP fellowship as the source of earlier research.
- Le Bras's [Les lieux du soin](https://doi.org/10.3917/pouv.196.0029), indexed abstract read, explains the extension of psychiatric care beyond asylums and the accompanying reorganisation of coercion. Combined with Génard's [Harvard project description](https://histecon.fas.harvard.edu/ehppf/Elsa_Genard.html), it supports a question about the everyday social operation of institutions. Their shared editorial and seminar-report authorship is direct evidence of collaboration on those texts; thematic proximity alone is not.
