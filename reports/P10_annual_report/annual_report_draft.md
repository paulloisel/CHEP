## DRAFT page 1 — A year of questions, sources and exchange

The Centre for History and Economics in Paris brings historical inquiry into conversation with the study of economic life. Based at the Sciences Po Centre for History, CHEP was established in 2022, following collaboration begun in 2019 with the Cambridge–Harvard Joint Centre. Its remit makes room for environmental history, legal and digital sources, and the history of economic ideas and experience. This annual prototype follows the academic year, not the calendar year. [CHEP overview](https://www.histecon.fr/en/), P10-S002; [Sciences Po institutional page](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/en/research/centre-for-history-and-economics-in-paris-chep/), P10-S004.

Three editorial conversations organise the pages that follow: **authority in everyday life; property and emancipation; and environmental transformation and its social costs**. These are interpretive connections across selected research, not official programme names or claims of collaboration among the featured researchers. A prison history, a study of colonial compensation and a project on forest planting ask different questions, yet each makes economic relations visible through particular institutions, people and sources. Publication, ongoing research, scholarly editing and discussion all belong in that account.

The record also includes research support. A January 2026 announcement names four doctoral grant recipients; their projects appear on page 5. The programme spread distinguishes listed activities from events with explicit retrospective documentation. It also separates Paris activity from participation in a shared network. These distinctions allow the report to recognise real contributions without converting a calendar entry into an attendance figure or attributing an entire partner institution's work to CHEP. [Sciences Po award announcement](https://www.sciencespo.fr/ecole-recherche/en/news/four-young-history-researchers-chep-2026-grant/), P10-S312.

## DRAFT page 2 — Research feature 1: Reading authority through prison lives

Elsa Génard’s *Sous les verrous. Une histoire sociale de la prison (France, premier XXe siècle)* makes everyday confinement an entry point into social history. Her September 2025 publication announcement describes research built from prison registers, individual files, reports and correspondence. These records reveal institutions through the lives that passed through them, connecting administrative routines with work, punishment and family relationships. The book’s contents place workshop hierarchies beside wartime imprisonment and the prospects of release. [Author’s announcement](https://www.asso-h2c.fr/2025/09/12/parution-elsa-genard-sous-les-verrous-une-histoire-sociale-de-la-prison/), P10-S105.

The method matters as much as the setting. Counting and comparing records sits alongside reconstructing individual trajectories and interpreting administrative language. Read this way, a prison is a place where wider social inequalities can be examined closely. For an economic-history readership, the attention to labour and resources offers a route into the relationship between institutions and ordinary experience. That connection is an editorial reading of the published description, not a claim to have reviewed the full book.

Génard also coedited the 2025 *Tracés* issue *Épreuves d’autorité*, whose publisher presents authority as a relationship made and tested through social encounters. The juxtaposition invites readers to connect a historical monograph with collective conceptual work. [ENS Éditions](https://catalogue-editions.ens-lyon.fr/fr/livre/?GCOI=29021100400380), P10-S112.

CHEP records Génard as a fellow in 2021–2022. This page therefore celebrates scholarship by a former fellow; it does not claim that these publications were funded by CHEP or written during that fellowship. [Past fellows](https://histecon.fr/en/research_fellows_past.html), P10-S104.

## DRAFT page 3 — Research feature 2: After abolition, following land and compensation

What can financial records reveal about the remaking of a colonial society? Jessica Balguy’s research follows compensation paid to former slave owners after the French abolition of slavery in 1848. Her earlier work examined recipients in Martinique, including owners classified as people of colour, using a database to connect individuals, property and the contested meanings of emancipation. The CIRESC account places this scholarship within the REPAIRS research programme. [CIRESC research profile](https://esclavages.cnrs.fr/lequipe/ancien-ne-s-doctorant-e-s/balguy-jessica/), P10-S020.

At CHEP, her institutional profile describes a further investigation of land around abolition: its redistribution after compensation and its subsequent use, ownership and labour. Economic data gathered in earlier research supplies a starting point for asking how colonial property relations changed and persisted. This is a description of an ongoing research agenda, not an announcement of completed findings. [Sciences Po profile](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/balguy-jessica/), P10-S016.

The project connects the reading of administrative evidence with questions that remain publicly contested. Balguy’s 2020 book brought together debates surrounding compensation, including legislative deliberations and competing claims. It is background to the present research, rather than a publication of this reporting year. [CIRESC publication notice](https://esclavages.cnrs.fr/indemniser-lesclavage-en-1848/), P10-S019.

Within the annual window, the September 2025 *Histoires de la vie économique* event identified Balguy with CHEP and CHSP. Her contribution makes a useful bridge between research on economic institutions and the close study of colonial social relations. The event record establishes an announced contribution; it does not by itself demonstrate delivery or audience response. [Dated seminar notice](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/evenements/vie-economique-20252026-1/), P10-S320.

## DRAFT page 4 — Research feature 3: When planting forests changes social relations

Lina Skoglund’s project on the Landes de Gascogne asks why responses to environmental transformation vary within a region. Her accessible working-paper abstract studies nineteenth-century afforestation, when a state-backed development project replaced common marshlands with pine plantations. It connects changes in landownership with resistance, commercial opportunities and the authority of local elites. [Author’s research abstract](https://linaskoglund.github.io/), P10-S015.

The abstract contrasts plantations acquired by urban investors with land controlled by established local owners. It argues that local elites could negotiate informal arrangements with poorer residents, while outside investors faced destructive resistance. The research combines commune-level evidence on ownership and fires with archival surveys and petitions. The point is not that environmental change produces one inevitable social reaction: the project examines how relationships around property shape the distribution of costs and conflict.

For this annual report, that question links environmental history to economic and political history without treating them as separate subjects. It also illustrates the value of moving between quantitative patterns and close archival investigation. These connections are editorial interpretation; the paper remains work in progress.

Sciences Po dates Skoglund’s CHEP visit from 1 December 2025 to 31 August 2027 and associates it with the joint environmental-history programme. The 2025–2026 seminar calendar scheduled the Landes research for 27 January 2026. Those records anchor the project within this year while leaving the paper’s precise version and the seminar’s delivery to be confirmed. [Visitor record](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/le-centre/chercheurs/chercheurs-invites/), P10-S314; [seminar programme](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/recherche/seminaires/seminaire-des-axes-et-des-domaines/histoires-de-la-vie-economique/), P10-S202.

## DRAFT page 5 — Junior researchers: four routes into economic life

The four 2026 doctoral grant recipients were publicly announced on 28 January 2026: **Francesco Giosuè, Rébecca Mathiot, Giulio Piumelli and Aesa Virely**. The following short descriptions draw on public project profiles, not interviews or reports of completed grant-funded results. The announcement establishes the award cohort; it does not identify research expenses, travel undertaken or individual award amounts. [Sciences Po announcement](https://www.sciencespo.fr/ecole-recherche/en/news/four-young-history-researchers-chep-2026-grant/), P10-S312; [CHEP research students](https://histecon.fr/en/research_students.html), P10-S302.

**Francesco Giosuè — Ideas moving through networks.** Giosuè studies transnational liberal connections between the Atlantic Revolutions and the Restoration, with attention to the Monarchiens and Doctrinaires. His project offers a route from political vocabulary to the people and networks through which it travelled. The CHEP profile also identifies banking connections within that history. [Sciences Po profile](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/giosue-francesco/), P10-S318; [CHEP profile](https://histecon.fr/en/Francesco_Giosue.html), P10-S309.

**Rébecca Mathiot — Risk, work and protection at sea.** Mathiot investigates occupational risks and social protection among seafarers in France, from the late nineteenth century to the 1950s. Her project brings maritime work into the history of welfare and asks how protection developed around a particular working environment. [Research profile](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/mathiot-rebecca/), P10-S315.

**Giulio Piumelli — Making the Washington Consensus.** Piumelli’s research follows the formation and implementation of the Washington Consensus between 1979 and 1995, examining interactions among US officials, international financial institutions and debtor states. It places an influential policy vocabulary within a history of institutions and international relationships. [Research profile](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/en/directory/piumelli-giulio/), P10-S316.

**Aesa Virely — Care across the life course.** Virely investigates children's and older people's homes in socialist Yugoslavia, 1945–1991. The project connects social protection with age and institutional life, offering another perspective on how societies organised care. [Research profile](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/virely-aesa/), P10-S317.

Together, these projects suggest connections across ideas, work, policy and care. This grouping is an editorial invitation to read across fields, not evidence that the researchers collaborate. A useful future edition would add one verified research milestone and one source or method chosen by each researcher, keeping the distinction between doctoral work in progress and a completed publication.

**Editorial query P10-Q05:** invite each recipient, through an authorised institutional process, to verify a 60-word project description and supply one dated milestone. Ask whether and how the grant helped; do not supply an imagined testimonial. Master's participation is not established by this doctoral award notice.
