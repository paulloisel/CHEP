# Histories of economic life

## Five years since CHEP’s establishment, 2022–2027

### Opening — 300 words

An anniversary can be an invitation to read together. The Centre for History and Economics in Paris brings a question into focus: how can historical research make economic life intelligible across different places, institutions, and experiences? This retrospective offers routes through that question, following publications featured by the centre and selected work by researchers connected with its community.

The chronology has more than one beginning. Collaboration between Sciences Po and the Cambridge and Harvard centres began in 2019, and a workshop that June served as a launch. Institutional accounts identify establishment in 2022. A celebration in 2027 would therefore mark five years since establishment, while recognising the earlier conversations from which the centre developed. This preparation draft stops at evidence available on 15 November 2026.

The works gathered here move between concepts and encounters. They examine credit and public power, property and empire, solidarity and confinement, and the evidence through which ordinary lives become historically visible. These headings are editorial invitations. They do not imply that the authors share one theory, influenced one another, or conducted all their research at CHEP.

Books sit beside articles, chapters, historical source editions, and public essays. Translations appear with their originals, making intellectual circulation visible without counting the same work twice. Earlier scholarship by a later affiliate is identified as such. A publication featured by the centre is not automatically a publication funded by it.

Each section proposes a conversation rather than a verdict. Readers can follow the bibliography to a publisher, an abstract, or an accessible text, and return with different connections of their own. Students have a role in this process: testing categories, questioning the sources, and identifying missing perspectives. The aim is a useful shared reading space, where the celebration of scholarship also opens possibilities for further inquiry, discussion, and exchange.

*Opening evidence:* [CHEP launch account, *A Spatial Turn in Economic History?*](https://histecon.fr/en/spatial.html); [Sciences Po, *Centre for History and Economics in Paris*](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/en/research/centre-for-history-and-economics-in-paris-chep/). ### 1. Capitalism, credit and public power

What happens when an economic category becomes an object of historical inquiry? This section moves from the vocabulary of economic life to institutions that organise borrowing and distribute authority. A conceptual book, an edited collection, a jointly written chapter and a journal response give readers different ways to approach those questions. The Todd–Yates chapter explicitly examines the connection between public borrowing and citizenship; its coauthorship is a documented connection. Pairing these texts with Levy or Trivellato is our editorial proposal. The invitation is to compare how concepts and practices meet, while allowing disagreement over the meaning of markets, obligations and public power.

*Read next:* P11-BW002, P11-BW005, P11-BW007, P11-W003, P11-W004, P11-W009 and P11-JW010. *Argument evidence:* [Todd and Yates, chapter abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-48794-2_4). Selection and metadata: [selection.csv](selection.csv). No intellectual influence is inferred from this grouping.

### 2. Property, empire and environments

Economic relations take place somewhere, but their boundaries rarely coincide neatly with a map. Property connects buildings and land with legal and political institutions. Imperial relationships can cross territorial jurisdictions, and environmental histories unsettle an account confined to transactions between people. This section proposes a reading path between urban space, transimperial lives, informal power, compensation and water. Its central question is methodological: what changes when historians follow a material resource or a disputed claim across scales? The texts remain distinct in place, period and argument. Bringing them together is an invitation to compare the evidence needed to describe possession, dependence and transformation.

*Read next:* P11-W002, P11-W007, P11-W008, P11-BW006 and P11-JW001. *Argument evidence:* [Yates, publisher summary](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108850551); [Oualdi, Columbia University Press description](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/a-slave-between-empires/9780231191869/). Amrith’s selected paperback is a 2020 edition of a work first published in 2018; it is not a new establishment-era output.

### 3. Solidarity and social institutions

Who can act together, and what limits the resources available to a person? The publications in this section approach those questions through different settings. Labour internationalism gives one route into collective organisation. Histories of prisons and asylums invite attention to institutional categories, family relationships and constrained lives. The editorial pairing does not equate these experiences or attribute a common explanation to the authors. Instead, it offers a way to discuss how historians move between rules, material circumstances and personal trajectories. A useful conversation might begin with a single document: what relationship does it record, whose voice does it preserve, and which obligations remain outside its frame?

*Read next:* P11-W001, P11-BW001 and P11-BW003. *Argument evidence:* [Other Press, *Struggle and Mutual Aid*](https://otherpress.com/product/struggle-and-mutual-aid-9781635420104/), and the author/publisher sources under the two confinement cards. This is an editorial comparison, not evidence of collaboration.

A methods guide, a family history and a student’s public essay suggest different scales of inquiry. Their juxtaposition invites readers to think about selection as part of research: deciding which traces belong together, which differences matter, and which conclusions exceed the sources. ## Six publication cards

Each card below contains 150 words of main copy, followed by evidence and relationship notes. No full-book reading is claimed. The wider eighteen-work selection is in [selection.csv](selection.csv), including the junior source edition, article and student essay; the six longer cards are a deliberately smaller prototype.


## P11-BW001 — Living within prison’s rules

**Elsa Génard, *Sous les verrous. Une histoire sociale de la prison (France, premier XXe siècle)* (Éditions de l’EHESS, 2025).**

What becomes visible when a prison is studied through everyday encounters? Elsa Génard reconstructs confinement in early twentieth-century France from administrative records, correspondence, and the traces of individual lives. The author’s presentation and contents foreground work, punishment, family relationships, wartime disruption, and release. Counting and close reading belong to the same inquiry: patterns across records help recover experiences that institutional categories can conceal. The book thus offers an entry into economic history through people whose opportunities and resources were sharply constrained.

For this retrospective, its editorial value lies in connecting material life to the routines that distribute authority. Read alongside *Aliénés*, it invites comparison of how confinement changes relationships beyond institutional walls. That pairing is a proposed conversation, not evidence of collaboration. A student contribution could trace how one type of document supports both a collective portrait and a personal trajectory, while identifying the silences that remain in the record.

*Evidence:* [Author’s publication announcement, description and contents](https://www.asso-h2c.fr/2025/09/12/parution-elsa-genard-sous-les-verrous-une-histoire-sociale-de-la-prison/) (12 September 2025), and [institutional bibliographic notice](https://univ-droit.fr/recherche/actualites-de-la-recherche/parutions/59855-sous-les-verrous), accessed 15 November 2026. Sources P11-BS005–006. **Relationship:** former CHEP fellow, 2021–2022; 2025 publication featured by the centre; support not established.

## P11-BW002 — What is an economy?

**Jonathan Levy, *The Real Economy: History and Theory* (Princeton University Press, 2025).**

Economic historians encounter categories that seem self-evident until their histories are examined. Jonathan Levy makes the economy itself the object of this questioning. The publisher’s account describes an argument drawing especially on Veblen and Keynes, addressing capitalism, uncertainty, demand, money, corporations, and globalization. Its ambition is to reconnect historical investigation with theoretical disputes about what economic life comprises. The book offers a starting point for readers who want concepts to remain open to revision through historical evidence.

Within this retrospective, the proposed conversation is between conceptual definitions and the practices recovered by other authors: borrowing, working, owning, and maintaining relationships. The connection is editorial, not a claim that these scholars share a theory. Students could bring a category from their own archival research and ask what it makes visible, what it excludes, and whether its meaning changes across time. That exercise would make a theoretical book useful across research stages.

*Evidence:* [Princeton University Press, *The Real Economy*, overview and publication details](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691252551/the-real-economy), accessed 15 November 2026; P11-BS008. **Relationship:** featured participant publication; author listed as CHEP Coordinator on the checked organisation page, without a documented start date in this source set.

## P11-BW003 — Confinement beyond the institution

**Anatole Le Bras, *Aliénés. Une histoire sociale de la folie au XIXe siècle* (CNRS Éditions, 2024).**

An asylum’s history can be told through its rules and administrators, but Anatole Le Bras approaches it through the trajectories of people confined there. The publisher’s description follows experiences from the manifestation of mental distress to internment, guardianship, relations with relatives, and life after release. Families, neighbours, police, and local officials appear in decisions that could alter a person’s rights and social position. Attention to accommodation and resistance makes confinement a relationship extending beyond the institution.

The editorial invitation is to compare this social history with Génard’s prison research without collapsing the differences between psychiatric and penal institutions. Both can prompt questions about how documentary categories frame lives, how dependence is produced, and which connections survive separation. A student-led discussion could compare the questions one might ask of an admission register and a family letter, considering what each source can establish and what evidence would be needed for a claim.

*Evidence:* [CNRS Éditions, *Aliénés*, presentation and details](https://www.cnrseditions.fr/catalogue/histoire/alienes/), accessed 15 November 2026; P11-BS009. **Relationship:** featured publication by a documented CHEP fellow. Fellowship dates remain conflicting across the home page, past-fellows page, and publisher biography; do not claim verified publication during affiliation.

## P11-BW004 — Following ordinary connections

**Emma Rothschild, *An Infinite History* (Princeton University Press, 2021), and *De proche en proche* (Seuil, 2025).**

Two documents concerning Marie Aymard and her family in Angoulême become the beginning of a wider inquiry. Emma Rothschild follows an extended family across five generations, relating ordinary connections to social and economic change. The companion website makes the investigation explorable through family trees, maps, and visualizations of the people linked to a marriage contract. In an interview, Rothschild explains how work in local archives and online collections developed from those discoveries. The French translation gives the same underlying work another route to readers.

For the retrospective, this is a conversation about scale and method. How can a small documentary starting point sustain inquiry across places and generations without turning one family into a representative sample? Students could examine a companion visualization and identify the distinction between a documented connection and an inferred relationship. The exercise would emphasize that a map is an argument whose evidential foundations must remain visible.

*Evidence:* [Book companion website](https://www.infinitehistory.org/en/), [Princeton overview](https://press.princeton.edu/books/audio/9780691215242/an-infinite-history), and [author interview and French-edition bibliography](https://www.rfgenealogie.com/infos/de-proche-en-proche), accessed 15 November 2026; P11-BS010–012. **Relationship:** both editions featured by CHEP; this establishes a participant-publication connection, not Paris funding. The map is linked, not reproduced.

## P11-BW005 — Credit, stories, and exclusion

**Francesca Trivellato, *The Promise and Peril of Credit* (Princeton University Press, 2019), translated as *Juifs et capitalisme* (Seuil, 2023).**

Financial instruments circulate with stories about who can be trusted. Francesca Trivellato investigates a false account attributing the invention of bills of exchange to Jews. Her introduction connects the operation of credit to the texts that carried this legend. It also describes how searchable collections helped trace references across works, while warning that digitization, language coverage, and imperfect recognition limit what such collections can establish. The inquiry brings economic practices and cultural representations into the same field of investigation.

The proposed editorial connection is to other works on the social boundaries of markets: inclusion can coexist with durable suspicion and inequality. A useful student exercise would distinguish evidence for a financial transaction from evidence for a stereotype circulating about it. The French edition and the author’s response to an *Annales* forum also make translation and scholarly disagreement visible as part of a publication’s continuing life, rather than merely bibliographic duplication.

*Evidence:* [Publisher-hosted introduction, especially pp. 1–3 and 8–10](https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/i13333.pdf), and [author’s 2024 response, abstract and reference 1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annales-histoire-sciences-sociales/article/abs/toutes-les-dettes-ne-se-valent-pas/064B43498A1F4D3A1C20BB744615C260), accessed 15 November 2026; P11-BS016, P11-BS018. **Relationship:** both books featured by CHEP; the response is separately labelled reception context, not a verified CHEP-supported output.

## P11-BW006 — Empire through economic relations

**David Todd, *A Velvet Empire* (Princeton University Press, 2021), translated as *Un empire de velours* (La Découverte, 2022).**

Empire does not always announce itself through a change of flag. David Todd examines the economic and cultural means through which France extended its influence after Napoleon’s defeat. The publishers’ descriptions identify luxury goods, lending, commercial arrangements, legal privileges, and military intervention as parts of this informal imperial power. They emphasize relationships with local elites and cooperation between European empires. The argument challenges an account of nineteenth-century France confined to withdrawal followed by renewed territorial conquest.

For this retrospective, the editorial opportunity is to connect international hierarchy with the institutions and transactions that sustain it. How should historians recognize coercion when sovereignty remains in place? A student-led comparison could set a legal privilege beside a commercial relationship and ask what evidence would establish dependence. Linking the English original and French translation would also show how a work travels between scholarly publics, while counting it once in the inventory of ideas.

*Evidence:* [Princeton University Press, overview and details](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691171838/a-velvet-empire), and [La Découverte, French edition](https://www.editionsladecouverte.fr/un_empire_de_velours-9782348073359), accessed 15 November 2026; P11-BS014–015. **Relationship:** both editions featured by CHEP; dated Paris affiliation and institutional support not established in this source set.

**Jessica Balguy, *Indemniser l’esclavage en 1848 ?* (2020), P11-JW001.** This edition of historical debates brings disputes over compensation into the reading list through sources rather than a conventional monograph. Its publisher biography gives a Harvard affiliation; publication during CHEP affiliation is unverified. It offers a student-authored example of making a historical debate accessible in a short format. The essay predates the documented 2026 CHEP student listing.
