## Preferred shortlist contributions

**Elizabeth Chatterjee.** Her comparative article argues that energy dependence helps explain the mobilization of US owner-operator truckers and North Indian farmers. By comparing groups between wage labour and large capital, it shows how demands for protection can reinforce carbon-intensive systems. Proposed question: *When does a claim to cheap energy become a class politics?* Intended audience: Master's/doctoral researchers in economic, social, environmental and South Asian history. Nicolas Delalande is a possible respondent on collective obligations and solidarity; Pierre Charbonnier on social protection and climate. Source basis: [Chicago profile](https://history.uchicago.edu/directory/elizabeth-chatterjee), [2024 open article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0010417524000069), [Delalande](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/delalande-nicolas/), [Charbonnier](https://www.sciencespo.fr/centre-etudes-europeennes/fr/annuaire/charbonnier-pierre/), accessed 2026-11-15. Read abstract, opening and concluding section; no claim to have closely read the entire article. Suggested activity: read the abstract and opening, then build a two-column comparison of ownership, dependence, state promise and protest tactic. USA travel/online timing, dates and fees remain unknown.

**Stefania Barca.** The accessible description of *Forces of Reproduction* treats subsistence and reproductive work as historical agency in sustaining life. Her later *Workers of the Earth* is a promising continuation for examining the boundary between industrial and unpaid work, although the available assessment of both books rests on descriptions rather than a full reading. Proposed question: *Whose work counts in environmental economic history, and what sources can make unpaid work visible?* Intended audience: labour, gender, environmental and economic historians. Suggested respondent: Nicolas Delalande, whose [author interview](https://www.sciencespo.fr/fr/actualites/nicolas-delalande/) discusses the historical construction and limits of worker solidarity. Sources: [current USC profile](https://investigacion.usc.gal/investigadores/190416/detalle?lang=en), [CUP book description](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/forces-of-reproduction/BE9B0DBDC89593F3284FE3F51D3B0418), [Pluto follow-up book](https://www.plutobooks.com/product/workers-of-the-earth/?select_location=US), all accessed 2026-11-15; Pluto indexed text retrieved but direct page 403. Suggested activity: use the CUP description as a ten-minute orientation, list two archive types that reveal or conceal nonwaged labour; for a substantive seminar obtain an author-approved excerpt through lawful access. Travel from Santiago de Compostela, language, fees and availability remain unknown.

**Oscar Sanchez-Sibony, third option.** The book argues that Soviet energy trading helped reshape international finance rather than remaining external to capitalist development. Its introduction offers a concrete way to connect material infrastructures to the formation of global institutions. Proposed question: *Did Soviet pipelines help make financial globalization?* [HKU profile](https://www.history.hku.hk/people/oscar-sanchez-sibony), [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108993555), [introduction excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811089/95184/excerpt/9781108995184_excerpt.pdf), accessed 2026-11-15. Only selected introduction passages and the publisher description were assessed. Suggested respondent: [Sabine Dullin](https://www.sciencespo.fr/histoire/fr/annuaire/dullin-sabine/), for Soviet transnational and imperial history. Activity: introduction pp. 7–8, draw a mechanism linking energy exports, dollar access and institutional change, then identify what evidence could challenge it. Hong Kong travel or online scheduling adds unresolved logistics.
